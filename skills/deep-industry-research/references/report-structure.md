# Deep Industry Research Report Structure

Build a detailed private-market investment roadmap. Keep the framework complete, but vary depth according to sector complexity and evidence. Core sections must satisfy the content depth contract in `SKILL.md`; minor or inapplicable sections may be concise when the reason is stated.

## Contents

- Part I: Investment view and scope
- Part II: Five-step industry core
- Part III: Investability and target map
- Part IV: Trigger-company implications and roadmap
- Sources and evidence quality

# Part I: Investment View and Scope

## 1. Executive Investment View

Lead with the decision:

- `积极看`, `选择性看`, `只看头部`, or `暂不看`;
- whether the sector deserves systematic coverage;
- most attractive and least attractive segments;
- suitable investment stage and check-size profile when inferable;
- core thesis and assumptions;
- strongest bear case;
- events or thresholds that invalidate the thesis;
- immediate sourcing, diligence, monitoring, or pass action.

Use a summary decision table:

| Decision dimension | Conclusion | Evidence | Investment implication | Confidence | What changes the view |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|

## 2. Industry Definition and Boundaries

- Define product, customer, use case, technology routes, geography, and value-chain scope.
- Explain adjacent markets and substitutes.
- State exclusions to prevent market-size double counting.
- Explain why the sector was triggered by the project or prior brief.
- Define technical and commercial terms used later.

# Part II: Five-Step Industry Core

## 3. Product, Customer Problem, and Technology Routes

Answer:

- What customer problem is solved?
- Which metrics determine customer selection?
- What are the main and substitute routes?
- How do routes compare on performance, cost, maturity, manufacturability, reliability, scalability, and adoption?
- Which routes suit which applications?
- What bottleneck limits each route?

Required analytical elements:

- technology-route selection matrix;
- commercialization stage for each important route;
- technology evolution timeline where route change matters;
- conclusion on likely winning routes by application;
- implications for investable layers and the trigger company.

Do not equate theoretical performance with a commercial moat.

## 4. Industry Stage, Demand Reality, and Inflection Points

Determine industry stage using more than market concentration:

- customer validation and repeat procurement;
- standards and qualification;
- production scale and yield;
- cost curve and ROI;
- supply-chain lock-in;
- competitive concentration;
- policy implementation and budget availability.

Explain:

- who pays and who uses;
- pain intensity and ROI;
- budget source and buying process;
- adoption and qualification cycle;
- current evidence: tenders, capex, design wins, purchase orders, deliveries, recognized revenue, collection, and repeat orders;
- false-demand traps;
- technology, demand, policy, cost, supply-chain, and capital-market inflections;
- events that open or close the investment window.

Required analytical elements:

- commercialization evidence ladder;
- industry-stage and inflection timeline;
- leading indicators and invalidation thresholds;
- strongest alternative explanation for apparent demand.

## 5. Value Chain, Cost Structure, and Profit Pool

Map:

- upstream equipment, materials, components, IP, and enabling technologies;
- midstream manufacturing, integration, platforms, and services;
- downstream applications, customers, channels, and payers;
- global and domestic representative players;
- bottleneck layers and localization gaps.

Analyze:

- component or service cost structure;
- price drivers and reduction paths;
- pricing power and customer bargaining power;
- gross-margin signals;
- capex and working capital;
- customer concentration;
- recurring or consumable revenue;
- learning curve and scale economics;
- layers that appear important but are structurally low-return.

Required analytical elements:

- value-chain map;
- cost breakdown or economics table when data permits;
- profit-pool comparison;
- investability conclusion by layer;
- trigger-company position and dependency risks.

## 6. Market Size and Growth Scenarios

Cover:

- TAM, SAM, and SOM;
- current global and China demand;
- applications and customer segments;
- historical and projected growth;
- conservative, base, and optimistic scenarios;
- adoption, volume, price, timing, and share assumptions;
- source disagreements and confidence;
- double-counting and boundary risks.

Required analytical elements:

- transparent bottom-up model or unit-based sanity check;
- top-down comparison where credible;
- market-scenario table with formulas, units, period, and geography;
- sensitivity to the 2–3 assumptions that matter most;
- explanation of the gap between industry TAM and startup-reachable revenue.

Do not treat an external forecast as a model.

## 7. Competition, Global Benchmarks, and Startup Window

Map:

- global leaders;
- China incumbents;
- focused startups;
- adjacent companies likely to enter;
- substitute routes;
- market shares when credible.

Compare:

- technology and product proof;
- manufacturability and scale;
- customer proof and qualification;
- cost and business economics;
- distribution, ecosystem, standards, patents, and supply chain;
- capital strength and acquisition capability.

Explain:

- incumbent advantages;
- startup entry windows;
- how consolidation may occur;
- what China can and cannot replicate from overseas;
- which competitor archetype threatens the trigger company most.

Required analytical elements:

- competition matrix;
- global benchmark table;
- entry-window and consolidation conclusion;
- evidence-based view of barriers rather than a generic moat list.

# Part III: Investability and Target Map

## 8. Primary-Market Financing and Valuation Heat

- Show financing activity by year, stage, and subsegment when data exists.
- Identify hot, neglected, and overheated areas.
- Compare typical valuation signals only when definitions are comparable.
- Identify active financial and strategic investors.
- Explain whether financing supports real commercial progress or narrative heat.

Use a table when enough transactions can be normalized:

| Company/segment | Date | Round | Amount | Valuation if known | Commercial milestone | Investor type | Signal |
|:---|:---|:---|---:|---:|:---|:---|:---|

## 9. Investable Segment Ranking

Rank segments using demand certainty, value capture, startup window, capital intensity, exit potential, timing, and risk.

| Rank | Segment | Demand certainty | Profit pool | Startup window | Capital intensity | Exit potential | Suitable stage | Diligence signal | Main risk |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|

For each top-ranked segment, explain:

- thesis;
- evidence;
- causal reasoning;
- investment implication;
- bear point;
- confidence and needed data.

## 10. Company Screening Standards

Define evidence thresholds for:

- founder and team;
- technology and IP;
- manufacturability and reliability;
- customer validation;
- revenue quality and collection;
- gross margin and unit economics;
- capex, working capital, and financing needs;
- governance and compliance;
- valuation and exit fit.

Separate:

- minimum proof before a meeting;
- proof before deep diligence;
- proof before investment committee;
- immediate red flags.

## 11. Potential Target Map

Include:

- private companies worth meeting;
- private companies worth deep diligence;
- listed companies to track;
- overseas benchmarks;
- strategic buyers or industrial investors;
- target archetypes to source proactively;
- companies or archetypes to pass quickly;
- important unknowns.

Use a target map:

| Company/archetype | Layer/route | Stage | Evidence of traction | Strategic fit | Why meet/pass | Key unknown | Next action |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|

## 12. Exit Path

- Analyze plausible IPO venues and requirements.
- Identify strategic buyers and acquisition logic.
- Compare public-market comps and secondary-market appetite.
- Explain likely consolidation timing.
- State exit bottlenecks, holding period, and conditions required for attractive returns.

Avoid presenting the existence of listed comps as proof of an exit path.

## 13. Risks, Bear Case, and Invalidation

Cover:

- demand and industry-cycle risk;
- route substitution;
- commercialization and scale-up;
- policy and geopolitical risk;
- competition and consolidation;
- capital intensity and financing;
- valuation and exit.

Develop the strongest bear case as seriously as the base thesis.

| Risk/assumption | Mechanism | Evidence | Impact | Leading indicator | Invalidation threshold | Action |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|

# Part IV: Trigger-Company Implications and Roadmap

## 14. Implication for the Trigger Company

Include only when research was triggered by a company or project.

Synthesize company implications already stated throughout the report:

| Industry success factor | Why it matters | Company position | Independent proof | Gap | Competitive consequence | Diligence action |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|

Conclude:

- whether sector research strengthens or weakens project interest;
- whether the company occupies a high-value layer;
- whether its route and timing are attractive;
- what must be verified next;
- whether better companies may exist in the same direction.

Do not turn this chapter into a full company-deep-dive.

## 15. Investment and Monitoring Roadmap

- Define what to track over the next 3–6 months.
- Identify companies and archetypes to source.
- List positive and negative market signals.
- Specify recurring data updates.
- Define triggers for company deep diligence.
- Define conditions for pausing or exiting sector coverage.
- Assign priority to expert calls, customer interviews, datasets, standards, or site visits needed next.

Use a tracking table:

| Signal | Current baseline | Update frequency | Positive threshold | Negative threshold | Source | Investment action |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|

# Sources and Evidence Quality

## 16. Source Inventory and Confidence

- List web sources with links.
- List local files with page or section references.
- Label important evidence High, Medium, or Low quality.
- Record source disagreements.
- Identify important unverified claims.
- Suggest additional evidence to obtain.
- Distinguish strongly supported conclusions from hypothesis-driven conclusions.

For major calculations, include an assumptions table or appendix sufficient for another analyst to reproduce the result.
